EfficientNetV2-Based Plant Leaf Disease Detection: A Multi-Phase Transfer Learning Approach for 46-Class Classification

Abstract

Plant diseases pose a significant threat to global food security, causing crop losses of up to 40% annually. Early and accurate detection of plant diseases is crucial for effective disease management and minimizing agricultural losses. This paper presents an automated plant leaf disease detection system using EfficientNetV2B0 architecture with a multi-phase transfer learning approach. Our system achieves 94.47% validation accuracy across 46 disease classes covering 14 crop types, with a compact model size of approximately 15 MB and inference time of ~50ms per image. The proposed system addresses the limitations of existing approaches through optimized preprocessing, label smoothing, and a three-phase training strategy. We demonstrate superior performance compared to baseline CNN models while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices.

1. Introduction

Agriculture forms the backbone of many economies worldwide, and plant diseases represent one of the most significant challenges to crop productivity. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), plant diseases cause annual crop losses of up to 40%, threatening global food security and the livelihoods of millions of smallholder farmers [1]. Traditional methods of disease identification rely heavily on expert knowledge, which is often unavailable in remote agricultural regions. The advent of deep learning has revolutionized image-based disease detection, offering automated, scalable, and accurate solutions [3].

Existing plant disease detection systems face several challenges, including limited disease coverage (most systems detect fewer than 20 disease classes), poor generalization from lab-controlled conditions to real-world field scenarios, high computational requirements demanding GPU infrastructure, and single-phase training strategies that lack optimization for fine-tuning [4][5]. These limitations hinder practical deployment in resource-constrained agricultural settings where such tools are most needed.

This paper presents an automated plant leaf disease detection system based on the EfficientNetV2B0 architecture [2] with a novel multi-phase transfer learning approach. Our system addresses these challenges by providing multi-class classification across 46 disease classes spanning 14 crop types, a three-phase progressive training strategy for optimal model convergence, CPU-optimized inference without GPU requirements, and an integrated disease information database with treatment and prevention recommendations. The proposed system achieves 94.47% validation accuracy with a compact model size of approximately 15 MB and inference time of ~50ms per image, making it suitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices in field conditions [7][8].

2.4 Addressing Existing Limitations

Our system addresses the key challenges identified in literature:

  1. Generalization: Use of EfficientNet preprocessing (-1 to 1 normalization) specifically designed for the architecture
  2. Accuracy plateau: Multi-phase training with progressive unfreezing

Phase 1: Transfer Learning (Warm-up)

  • Duration: 10 epochs
  • Learning Rate: 0.002
  • Strategy: Freeze base model, train only custom head
  • Expected Accuracy: ~85%

Phase 2: Fine-tuning

  • Duration: 15 epochs
  • Learning Rate: 0.0001
  • Strategy: Unfreeze top 50 layers of EfficientNetV2B0
  • Expected Accuracy: ~92%

Phase 3: Precision Training

  • Duration: Variable (until convergence)
  • Learning Rate: 1e-6
  • Strategy: Full model fine-tuning with very low learning rate
  • Achieved Accuracy: 94.47% (Peak: 96.50%)

5.3 Key Implementation Features

5.3.1 Label Smoothing for Better Generalization

model.compile(
    optimizer=tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.002),
    loss=tf.keras.losses.CategoricalCrossentropy(label_smoothing=0.1),
    metrics=['accuracy']
)

5.3.2 Loss Function and Label Smoothing

We employ categorical cross-entropy loss with label smoothing (\(\alpha\) = 0.1) to prevent overconfidence and improve generalization:

With label smoothing, the target distribution becomes:

\[y_{i}^{smooth} = (1 - \alpha) \cdot y_{i} + \frac{\alpha}{C}\]

Where \(C\) = 46 (number of classes), \(\alpha\) = 0.1 (smoothing factor), \(y_i\) is the one-hot encoded target, and \(\hat{y}_i\) is the predicted probability.

The cross-entropy loss is then computed as:

\[\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^{C} y_{i}^{smooth} \cdot \log(\hat{y}_{i})\]

5.3.3 Softmax Activation

The output layer uses softmax activation to produce probability distribution across all 46 disease classes:

\[\sigma(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}\]

Where \(z_i\) represents the raw output (logit) for class \(i\), and the denominator normalizes across all \(C\) = 46 classes.

5.3.4 Performance Metrics

Model performance is evaluated using standard classification metrics:

Accuracy measures overall correctness:

\[\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}\]

Precision measures the proportion of true positives among predicted positives for each class \(i\):

\[\text{Precision}_i = \frac{TP_i}{TP_i + FP_i}\]

Recall (Sensitivity) measures the proportion of actual positives correctly identified:

\[\text{Recall}_i = \frac{TP_i}{TP_i + FN_i}\]

F1-Score provides the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

\[F_1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}\]

Where TP denotes True Positives, TN denotes True Negatives, FP denotes False Positives, and FN denotes False Negatives.

5.3.5 Learning Rate Schedule

We employ ReduceLROnPlateau for adaptive learning rate adjustment:

\[lr_{new} = lr_{current} \times \gamma \quad \text{if } \Delta L_{val} < \epsilon \text{ for } p \text{ epochs}\]

Where \(\gamma\) = 0.5 (reduction factor), \(\epsilon\) is the minimum improvement threshold, and \(p\) = 3 (patience epochs). This strategy allows the model to escape local minima and converge to better solutions during later training phases.

6.6 Comparison with State-of-the-Art

Our EfficientNetV2B0-based system outperforms all baseline models in accuracy while maintaining a significantly smaller model footprint. 8. Conclusion

This paper presented a comprehensive plant leaf disease detection system using EfficientNetV2B0 with a novel multi-phase transfer learning approach. The proposed system achieves 94.47% validation accuracy across 46 disease classes covering 14 crop types, with a compact model size of approximately 15 MB and an inference time of ~50ms per image. Unlike existing approaches that rely on computationally expensive architectures such as VGG-16 (528 MB, 91%) or ResNet-50 (98 MB, 93%), our system delivers superior accuracy while maintaining a dramatically smaller footprint suitable for CPU-only deployment. The three-phase training strategy — comprising transfer learning warm-up, progressive fine-tuning, and precision training — proved essential for extracting maximum performance from the EfficientNetV2B0 backbone without catastrophic forgetting. Additionally, the integration of label smoothing (\(\alpha\) = 0.1) with categorical cross-entropy loss contributed to improved generalization across diverse disease categories. The system’s practical deployment as a Flask-based web application, complemented by a comprehensive disease information database providing treatment and prevention recommendations, demonstrates its readiness for real-world agricultural use. By enabling accurate, rapid, and accessible disease diagnosis without specialized hardware, this work contributes to the broader goal of leveraging artificial intelligence for global food security and sustainable agriculture [1][7][8].

9. Future Work

Several directions for future research and development emerge from this work. The model can be converted to TensorFlow Lite for mobile deployment, enabling offline disease diagnosis directly on smartphones in remote agricultural regions. Expanding the dataset to include over 100 disease classes and incorporating multi-spectral imaging data would improve coverage and robustness across diverse environmental conditions. Real-time video analysis and integration with IoT-enabled agricultural drones could enable large-scale field monitoring. Additionally, exploring Vision Transformer architectures and attention mechanisms may further improve classification accuracy, while federated learning approaches would allow collaborative model improvement across farming communities without compromising data privacy [13][14][15].
